The Government of Yukon's Department of Justice told a person who had been held at the Whitehorse Correctional Centre that no responsive surveillance recordings had been found and directed them to the RCMP instead.

By then, according to a later court judgment, the department had already accepted the request and collected **105 hours of video** by accessing an RCMP server. The department said its own copy had passed a 30-day retention period and that the remaining recordings were in federal custody and control.

That contradiction prompted the Yukon Information and Privacy Commissioner to open an investigation without waiting for a formal complaint. When the commissioner summoned five Justice employees for sworn interviews, the department and those employees filed two petitions asking the Supreme Court of Yukon to set the summonses aside.

The first petition was filed on August 21, 2025. The final decision came 320 days later, on July 7, 2026.

Chief Justice Suzanne Duncan dismissed both petitions.

  number={1}

>
  Justice argued that the employees had already provided everything they knew
  and that making them attend was an unnecessary hardship. The court found
  concrete unanswered questions, held that all five had relevant information and
  said the burden argument was unsupported by evidence.
</KeyFinding>

## What the records establish

This is not a story about a court finding corruption, bad faith or an access-law violation. No such findings were made.

It is a story about a government department asking a court to prevent its independent information watchdog from compelling five employees to answer relevant factual questions—and losing on the merits.

<EvidenceBox
  title="Two petitions sought to set aside five interview summonses"
  status="confirmed"
>
  The court heard the petitions together and dismissed both. It held that the
  commissioner's investigation was authorized, the requested information was
  reasonably relevant, the notices were sufficient and requiring the employees
  to testify was not an abuse of process.
</EvidenceBox>

The case arose from Access Request No. 25-042, which sought recordings from the Arrest Processing Unit at the Whitehorse Correctional Centre. The unit uses one video feed recorded on two servers. Justice retains recordings on one system for 30 days; the RCMP retains its copy for two years.

The court recorded the following sequence:

| Date              | Event                                                                  | What it established                                                                                           |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| June 11, 2025     | Justice issued its response to the requester.                          | It said no records were found, its retention period had expired and the requester could apply to the RCMP.    |
| July 3, 2025      | The IPC learned of Justice's position.                                 | The commissioner decided to examine the department's compliance on his own motion.                            |
| July 8, 2025      | The IPC sent a formal investigation notice.                            | The inquiry asked whether Justice had processed and answered Request No. 25-042 in accordance with ATIPPA.    |
| August 1, 2025    | Justice answered in writing.                                           | It supplied submissions and two statutory declarations, maintaining that the RCMP controlled the recordings.  |
| August 15, 2025   | The IPC summoned two employees.                                        | The commissioner sought sworn testimony about the request and the department's video-access process.          |
| August 21, 2025   | Justice and the first two employees petitioned the court.              | They asked the court to set aside the summonses.                                                              |
| September 2025    | The IPC summoned three more employees; a second petition followed.     | Five employee summonses were eventually before the court.                                                     |
| November 21, 2025 | The court refused to strike the first petition at a preliminary stage. | The challenge was legally arguable and entitled to a hearing on its merits.                                   |
| July 7, 2026      | The court released its merits decision.                                | Both petitions were dismissed; the IPC could proceed, subject to a technical amendment to its records demand. |

## A separate case had already answered a similar question

The IPC's concern was not based on Request No. 25-042 alone.

In a separate 2024 case, a lawyer sought video of a client detained in the same Arrest Processing Unit. Justice located 53 video files totalling about 27.5 hours, then withheld all of them. The IPC found that Justice held the recordings for the purposes of ATIPPA and could not use its agreement with the RCMP to avoid its own disclosure obligations.

On December 30, 2024, Deputy Minister and Deputy Attorney General Mark Radke wrote that Justice accepted all three recommendations. The department agreed to disclose the video after obscuring third parties. The 2026 judgment records that disclosure occurred.

These were **different access requests**, and the publicly available material does not establish that they involved the same requester or incident. What matters is the institutional position: Justice accepted the earlier finding that it held this kind of video, then took what the court called an apparent contradictory position in the new matter.

<KeyFinding
  number={2}
  title="The written evidence did not explain the apparent reversal"
>
  The court said the affidavits and declarations did not answer why Justice's
  new position differed from the recommendation it had accepted in the earlier
  case. They also did not explain how the department intended to handle similar
  requests in the future.
</KeyFinding>

The IPC wanted factual answers to questions including:

- If the RCMP had custody and control, how had Justice accessed 105 hours of recordings?
- Were there material differences between the two access requests?
- What had officials discussed about accessing video on the RCMP server?
- What did internal emails show about the department's response?
- What process would Justice use for similar requests in the future?

Chief Justice Duncan held that those questions were relevant to how and why Justice responded as it did, and therefore to whether it complied with the Act.

## Justice's case, in its strongest form

Justice did not argue that the IPC lacked authority to investigate altogether. Its case was more specific.

The department argued that the formal notice defined the inquiry too narrowly to support the questions the IPC wanted to ask. It said the commissioner was pursuing broader policy or legal issues for a collateral purpose. It said the employees' declarations and affidavits contained all of their relevant knowledge, that compelled attendance would impose an onerous burden, and that the IPC should have identified in advance the additional information it expected to obtain.

Justice also argued that demands for government records had to be directed to the head of the public body, not attached to summonses addressed to individual employees.

There was a legitimate legal question to adjudicate. In the November 2025 preliminary ruling, Chief Justice Duncan declined to strike the first petition, found that it had a reasonable chance of success and held that a petition was an appropriate procedure. That ruling did not validate Justice's arguments; it allowed them to receive a full merits hearing.

On the merits, almost all of those arguments failed.

## What the court decided

The court held that the investigation notice had to be read as a whole, in light of ATIPPA's purposes. Justice's proposed reading was too restrictive. Questions about the department's ability to process this and similar video requests were part of the authorized investigation, not an improper collateral inquiry.

The judgment also rejected the idea that the department could decide for the investigator that the witnesses had nothing more to offer. The IPC—not the department being investigated—was entitled to determine whether its fact-finding was complete, subject to court oversight.

The court identified the roles, emails, technical knowledge and future-planning discussions that connected each employee to the inquiry. It found that the written evidence left relevant factual questions unanswered.

<EvidenceBox
  title="The claimed burden was not supported by evidence"
  status="confirmed"
>
  Justice characterized compelled attendance as onerous because the employees
  had significant operational duties and, in its view, little additional
  information. The court said the government supplied no evidence for that
  burden argument and found that compliance would not be an abuse of process.
</EvidenceBox>

Justice did obtain one technical correction. The court held that the IPC was entitled to the specified records, but the production demand should be directed to the head of the Department of Justice rather than to individual employees. It allowed the commissioner to amend the summonses accordingly.

The court also recommended practical limits: tell each employee how long the interview was expected to take, and reconsider whether all five remained necessary after hearing from some of them.

Those points matter. This was not a judgment that the IPC's every procedural choice was perfect. It was a judgment that the interviews were lawful and relevant, with a fix to the addressee for document production.

## The watchdog's broader account—and its limits

The IPC's 2025 annual report describes a more confrontational dispute than the judgment resolves.

Commissioner Jason Pedlar wrote that Justice insisted the first two employees would attend only if accompanied by departmental lawyers. His office refused because it believed having lawyers from the department under investigation in the interviews created a conflict of interest. Pedlar said the litigation forced the investigation to pause.

In a broader section about legal barriers across the commissioner's mandates, Pedlar attributed Justice challenges to long delays, investigative barriers and **“tens of thousands of dollars in legal expenses.”** The report does not itemize that sum or establish how much was caused by this summons case rather than other disputes.

<EvidenceBox
  title="Pedlar alleges broader interference that the judgment did not decide"
  status="alleged"
>
  The commissioner's account of lawyer attendance, conflict and wider record
  refusals is an official allegation, not a finding in the July 2026 judgment.
  The confirmed points are narrower: two petitions were filed, the IPC said its
  inquiry was paused, and the court ultimately upheld the interview summonses.
</EvidenceBox>

The judgment does not decide whether departmental counsel could attend an IPC interview. It does not determine the amount or reasonableness of either office's legal costs. And it does not adopt Pedlar's characterization of Justice's conduct as interference.

Those distinctions do not make the broader account unimportant. They define what still needs evidence.

## What the court did not decide

<EvidenceBox
  title="The underlying access-to-information case remains unresolved"
  status="unresolved"
>
  The court decided who could be questioned and what information could be
  sought. It did not decide whether Justice processed Request No. 25-042
  lawfully, whether the requester was entitled to the video, or whether any
  official acted in bad faith. No individual employee was found to have
  committed misconduct.
</EvidenceBox>

The IPC's public reports index did not list a completed report for the own-motion investigation when reviewed on September 10, 2026. It is therefore not yet possible to say what the witness interviews established, whether the department ultimately complied with ATIPPA, or what remedy—if any—the commissioner will recommend.

The Yukon Court of Appeal's published-judgment index also showed no appeal decision in this matter as of that date. That is not proof that no notice of appeal was filed. The registry or the parties would need to confirm the procedural status.

## The unanswered accountability question

The court record explains Justice's legal theory. It does not explain the public-administration decision behind it.

Why did the department decide that two court petitions were a better response than letting five employees answer the commissioner's relevant questions, while preserving specific objections as they arose?

That question cannot responsibly be answered from motive or speculation. It requires records and a response. At minimum, the public should be able to learn:

- who authorized each petition and what alternatives were considered;
- the legal costs incurred by Justice and the IPC, separated by proceeding;
- why Justice's position on control of APU video changed—or what factual difference it says explains the change;
- whether the five interviews occurred after the July ruling;
- how Justice now processes requests for APU recordings; and
- whether Request No. 25-042 has been reconsidered or remains denied.

<KeyFinding number={3} title="The clean finding is institutional, not personal">
  A department under investigation tried to have a court cancel its watchdog's
  interview summonses. After a full hearing, the court found the questions
  relevant, the witnesses connected to the issues and the claimed burden
  unsupported. The merits of the original access request—and the department's
  reason for choosing litigation—remain open.
</KeyFinding>

## Why it matters

An information commissioner cannot test a department's account only by accepting the material that department chooses to submit. The power to identify and question relevant witnesses is what turns oversight into investigation.

The context makes that power especially important. The records concerned continuous surveillance of a person held in custody. Justice's own retention period was only 30 days, while the RCMP copy lasted two years. A requester sent from one institution to another can lose time while control of the record is debated.

The July decision now provides a Yukon precedent: a statutory investigator may seek information that is reasonably relevant to a lawful inquiry, and the investigated public body does not get the final word on whether fact-finding is complete.

That is the verified accountability result. Whether the investigation ultimately finds an access-law breach is the next story, not a conclusion this one can supply in advance.
