Getting what you ordered is not proof you got a good deal.

That is the problem at the heart of Yukon’s 2024 purchasing audit. All 53 contracts in its representative sample delivered what government ordered. But in 27 files, auditors found no evidence that officials had checked the market before choosing a supplier.

For taxpayers, that leaves a basic question unanswered: how did government know the purchase made sense? For local and Yukon First Nations businesses, the missing records make it harder to see whether other suppliers were considered.

The question matters more now. On September 1, 2026, Yukon raised the limits for buying without a competitive tender.

## The missing step was shopping around

The [Auditor General of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/auditor-general/our-work/audit-reports/yuk-202411-e.html) examined purchasing across 16 Yukon government organizations. They had issued 47,041 contracts worth just over $1.2 billion during the period examined.

Auditors did not read every file. They selected a representative sample of 53 and tested how officials had planned, awarded and managed those contracts.

In 27 of the 53, the files did not show that officials had researched the market before the award: what suppliers were available and what they could offer. That missing evidence is why the audit said competition and value for money were not demonstrated.

The government’s defence was that the shopping may have happened even when nobody wrote it down. Departments told auditors that at least some market research had gone unrecorded. In its [public response](https://yukon.ca/en/news/statement-minister-clarke-office-auditor-general-canadas-performance-audit-government-yukon), Yukon described the problem as documentation rather than purchasing itself.

That is a possible explanation. It is also the reason the records matter.

At the [January 2025 legislative hearing](https://yukonassembly.ca/sites/default/files/2025-01/pac-35-public-hearing-transcript-2025-01-16.pdf), auditors explained that they were not demanding elaborate reports for small purchases. A handwritten note, an email or a list of prices collected by phone could be enough. Bigger, more complicated purchases called for more detail.

<KeyFinding title="A missing note can leave a public decision impossible to check">
  Without a record of the market research, the public cannot test why one
  supplier was chosen over another. The purchase may have been sensible.
  Government still needs evidence that lets someone check.
</KeyFinding>

## What the audit did—and did not—find

The purchases arrived. They met the stated needs. All 53 representative files followed the rules governing when a tender was required. The auditors found no fraud and no real or perceived conflicts of interest in the files reviewed.

Those findings matter. So does the difference between receiving an item and checking the deal before ordering it.

In a small or remote community, there may be very few suppliers. A direct award—choosing a supplier without running a competitive tender—can be reasonable. The government made that point, and the audit did not find that every purchase needed a formal competition.

But a small market can still be described in a note. Who could supply the item? What alternatives were considered? Why did this choice make sense?

The audit did not put a dollar figure on overpayment or waste. **Twenty-seven of 53 files lacked proof of market research. That does not mean half of $1.2 billion was wasted.**

The audit used two different samples. Their results cannot be mixed together:

| Contracts examined                                 | Number | Total contract value | Result                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -----: | -------------------: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Representative sample                              |     53 |             $535,106 | 27 files lacked evidence of market research before the award.             |
| Separate sample of large contracts chosen for risk |     10 |          $60,961,664 | One failed the audit’s competition and value-for-money test; nine passed. |

The representative sample was designed to support conclusions about the wider group of contracts, within statistical limits. The 10 large contracts were deliberately selected for closer scrutiny. Their results cannot be applied to all large contracts.

<details>
<summary>The sampling and spending figures, in detail</summary>

The full group comprised 47,041 contracts with start and end dates between April 1, 2019 and October 31, 2023, worth $1,209,935,841. The representative sample was randomized across all 16 organizations and included at least one file from each. The audit reports at least 90% confidence and a one-sided margin of error no greater than 10 percentage points for its population conclusions.

The 27 files that failed the test had a combined contract value of about $149,598. That is the value of the contracts, not an amount proven lost. The whole 53-contract sample was worth $535,106, less than one-twentieth of 1% of the full group’s contract value.

Government spent $452,817 against that sample’s contract value and $60.48 million against the large-contract sample’s $60.96 million. Staying within a contract ceiling does not itself establish that the price was competitive.

Fifty-two of the 53 representative contracts were below $50,000; 69% of those were directly awarded. Across the full group, 94% of contracts were below $50,000 and 73% of contracts below that amount were directly awarded.

Sixteen of the 27 files without market-research evidence came from the COVID-19 period. The auditors did not separately test the pandemic’s effects, found compliant files from the same period and identified no timing pattern. The pandemic was context, not an established explanation for the result.

</details>

## Yukon has raised the limits

From September 1, officials can make larger purchases without a competitive tender:

| Type of purchase | Previous limit | New limit |
| ---------------- | -------------: | --------: |
| Goods            |        $10,000 |   $18,000 |
| Services         |        $50,000 |   $75,000 |
| Construction     |        $50,000 |  $100,000 |

The government says the [higher limits](https://yukon.ca/en/news/government-yukon-increases-purchasing-limit-direct-award-contracts) reflect inflation, reduce administrative work and make it easier for Yukon businesses to get contracts. Staff received guidance. Further inflation adjustments are planned every two years, starting in 2028.

There is a sensible case for avoiding the time and cost of a pointless tender. The increase does not prove that the next purchase will be wasteful.

It does give officials more room to choose a supplier without a formal competition. Our assessment is that this makes a clear record of the choice more important: what the market offered, why the supplier was selected and who approved it.

Permission to skip a tender is not permission to skip explaining the purchase.

## Departments could reject advice without giving a reason

The audit also found a hole in the system meant to catch risky purchases.

Government organizations referred 291 proposed purchases to Highways and Public Works for advice because they might conflict with purchasing policy. In 110 cases—38%—the advice was not accepted. Most of the concerns involved proposals to award contracts directly above the usual limit.

Department heads were allowed to reject that advice if they considered it in their organization’s best interests. They had to notify Highways and Public Works. They did not have to explain why or later report whether the purchase achieved its goal.

The central department also relied on other departments to flag problems. It could not know whether every purchase needing advice had been referred, and it did not bring together the results of cases where its advice was rejected.

That left oversight with a question it could not answer: when a department chose to go ahead anyway, how did it turn out?

A [January 2025 update](https://yukonassembly.ca/sites/default/files/2025-01/pac-35-submission-2024-01-23-hpw-written-responses.pdf) described 97 newer assessment files. Advice was accepted in 25, overturned in 27, and the proposal was withdrawn in 25; 20 were still being processed. Those figures show activity. They do not show whether the completed purchases worked out.

The original 291 referrals were less than 1% of all contracts. They were flagged cases, not a measure of how often officials rejected advice across every purchase.

## Work first, signatures later

Four contracts in the representative sample were signed between two weeks and four months after work began.

A fifth, in the separate large-contract sample, went much further. Health and Social Services directly awarded a [dietary and food-services contract worth $1,934,874](https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/oag-bvg/2021-2024-reports/documents/yuk_202411_44587_e.pdf). Services began almost a year before the agreement was signed.

Auditors found no market research for that award. Highways and Public Works advised that the circumstances did not qualify for an exception to competitive tendering. Health and Social Services did not accept the advice.

Across the two samples, two of the five late contracts also lacked the written notices required for agreements signed after work began. Auditors checked the payments: none was made before a contract was signed.

## Counting contracts is not enough

The 16 organizations did not regularly and formally check whether their purchasing created competition or delivered value for money. Highways and Public Works held central data but had not worked out what information it needed to judge the policy’s results.

A five-year review was due in 2024. At the time of the audit, the department had neither conducted it nor made a plan to do so.

The same problem affected reporting on the Yukon First Nations Procurement Policy. Government counted activity, such as contracts awarded and labour involved, without showing whether the policy was achieving its goals.

Local participation was substantial: Yukon businesses received 77% of the contracts in the representative sample and 83% across the full group. Those figures do not answer whether the policy was producing the opportunities and outcomes it promised.

The [Yukon First Nation Caucus told legislators](https://yukonassembly.ca/sites/default/files/2025-05/pac-35-submission-2025-02-18-yfncaucus.pdf) that the audit had not adequately assessed the distinct First Nations policy or obligations under Chapter 22 of the Final Agreements. That is the caucus’s position, not an audit finding.

The audit’s test of contract monitoring for First Nations participation applied to only two representative files; both passed. This audit cannot stand as a complete verdict on the policy’s results.

## What government says it has fixed

Yukon accepted all five audit recommendations. They covered documenting decisions and signing before work starts, stronger conflict declarations, better departmental monitoring, information to assess policy outcomes, and tracking results when central advice is rejected.

There has been action. Highways and Public Works added documentation guidance, expanded conflict-of-interest certification and developed an [action plan](https://yukonassembly.ca/sites/default/files/2025-01/pac-35-submission-2024-01-16-hpw-action-plan-progress-update.pdf). In January 2026, Yukon also announced a [pilot to publish bid prices](https://yukon.ca/en/news/government-yukon-improve-procurement-processes-pilot-public-posting-bid-prices) after selected open competitions.

The latest detailed public progress account we located was a [department briefing note dated December 5, 2025](https://open.yukon.ca/information/1f39f8cb-c1ce-4fd9-85c1-f859fc636456/resource/edf52775-8b12-4f71-9d19-8f2a02b9c67b/download/hpw_fall_2025.pdf):

- **Two recommendations reported complete:** documentation and signing controls, and conflict certification.
- **Three still being worked on:** data and monitoring, measuring policy results, and following up on rejected advice.

Those are the department’s own claims. We have not independently verified completion.

Legislators [discussed a newer update on February 3, 2026](https://yukonassembly.ca/sites/default/files/2026-02/pac-36-minutes-2026-02-03.pdf), but the committee page checked on September 10 did not link the update itself. Its contents could not support a newer completion count in this article. That does not mean work stopped in December.

There is also an unanswered review promise. In 2024, the minister said an independent, comprehensive purchasing-policy review would happen in 2025. The primary records we reviewed did not establish that it was completed.

A separate [call for bids to evaluate the Yukon First Nations Procurement Policy](https://canadabuys.canada.ca/en/tender-opportunities/tender-notice/bt-8e5aafea-5761-4e3a-a940-04650f6fbfef) opened on August 27, 2026 and was still open when checked for this article. It seeks a nine-month evaluation. Commissioning that work is not the same as finishing it, and it does not settle the status of the broader review.

## Show whether the next file is better

Government can make the follow-through easier to judge:

- Publish the latest progress report, with evidence for each recommendation described as complete.
- Show whether checks of actual contract files now find market research and signatures before work begins.
- Report what happened when departments rejected central advice, including their reasons and whether the purchases achieved their goals.
- Publish how competition, value for money and Yukon First Nations policy outcomes are being measured.
- Explain what became of the comprehensive review promised for 2025.

The audit did not uncover a calculated loss. It uncovered decisions that government too often could not demonstrate were properly tested.

Yukon has raised the spending limits. Now it needs to show that the checks are working.
