After the Eagle Gold mine failure sent ore and cyanide solution down Dublin Gulch, a requester asked for government emails about the response. The disaster was near Mayo, on the Traditional Territory of the First Nation of Na-Cho Nyak Dun. Water, cleanup and public money were at stake.

Yukon’s information watchdog found that the Department of Environment had not justified withholding some records. It recommended releasing them. Environment rejected part of that recommendation.

Yukon law allowed it. The requester then had 30 business days to ask a court to review the refusal.

**Winning the watchdog’s backing did not guarantee getting the records.**

## What the requester wanted

The request covered emails between specified Environment and Energy, Mines and Resources staff about the government’s response to the June 24, 2024 failure. It sought correspondence from that day through November 21, 2024.

Environment released some information and withheld other material. The requester complained. After efforts to settle the dispute failed, an adjudicator for the Information and Privacy Commissioner—the IPC—examined the records and the department’s reasons for keeping them back.

The rule was straightforward: Environment had to prove that the law allowed it to withhold the information. For records identified in the [adjudicator’s final findings](https://yukonaccountability.ca/sites/default/files/reports/ATP-ADJ-2025-05-096%20Investigation%20Report%20%28revised%29%20--%2004%20Sep-25_Redacted.pdf), it had not done so.

That finding covered particular records and passages. It did not make every email public or sweep away every protection in the access law.

## Why Environment refused

Environment’s [August 28, 2025 response](https://yukonaccountability.ca/sites/default/files/reports/ATP-ADJ-2025-05-096%20Final%20Response%20Letter%20%28ENV%29_Redacted.pdf) described an urgent crisis. It said the government and the court-appointed receiver managing the company’s affairs needed to exchange information freely and quickly. Releasing unfinished or partial material, it argued, could cause misunderstanding and damage future cooperation.

The department also invoked legal privilege: the protection for confidential communications between lawyers and their clients. It said the decision to give up that protection belonged to the Attorney General and deputy Attorney General, not the watchdog.

The adjudicator accepted that some confidentiality served a real purpose. For a separate group of protected records, he found that the public interest did not justify overriding the protection. He recognized that misunderstood information could interfere with cleanup.

But a good reason for some confidentiality does not settle every redaction. For the passages he recommended releasing, the adjudicator found that Environment had not met the relevant legal tests. He also pointed to an existing way for government to receive information in confidence under Yukon’s access regulations.

The dispute was about whether particular information qualified for protection. The records do not establish a cover-up or a motive for withholding it.

## The next move fell to the requester

A watchdog recommendation and a court order are different things.

Under Yukon’s [Access to Information and Protection of Privacy Act](https://laws.yukon.ca/cms/images/LEGISLATION/PRINCIPAL/2018/2018-0009/2018-0009_2.pdf), a department has 15 business days to accept or reject each recommendation. If it rejects one, it must explain why and tell the complainant about the right to seek court review.

Environment’s letter did that. It accepted some recommendations, rejected others and notified the requester of the 30-business-day deadline. This was a refusal through a process the law provides, not defiance of a court order.

A court can order disclosure. It did so in a separate access case, [VinAudit Canada Inc. v Yukon](https://www.yukoncourts.ca/sites/default/files/2024-01/2023_yksc_68_vin_audit_v_yukon_corr1_0.pdf), after a department rejected a recommendation. The IPC can take part in such a case, but the requester has to start it.

That leaves a practical hurdle: the person seeking the records needs the time, means and willingness to go to court. The watchdog finding alone does not finish the job.

## Fourteen decisions are not fourteen pages

There is another problem with the public account: the unit being counted.

The [IPC’s reports page](https://yukonaccountability.ca/ipc/reports) says Environment continued withholding “14 of the 33 pages” recommended for release. Its 2025 annual report repeats that description.

We counted the entries in Environment’s response. There are 33 decision lines, 14 marked not accepted. But a line can cover several pages, a single passage or pages that appear elsewhere in the table.

| What the response table shows                      | Count | What that tells us                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ----: | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Decisions to accept or reject disclosure           |    33 | These are table entries, not a count of pages.                                     |
| Decisions marked not accepted                      |    14 | These identify the recommendations Environment rejected.                           |
| Different page numbers touched by those rejections |    47 | Some rejections concern only part of a page. This is not 47 wholly withheld pages. |

<KeyFinding title="The numbers match. The units do not.">
  Fourteen of 33 decision lines were rejected. The documents do not explain how
  that becomes 14 of 33 pages. They also do not show how much text remained
  withheld after Environment accepted the rest.
</KeyFinding>

That matters because “14 pages” sounds like a clear measure of what the requester was denied. The table cannot supply that measure. The watchdog should explain its count or correct it.

The rejected lines cover eight record groups. We expanded the page ranges and counted each page number once, including where the same pages appeared under different claimed protections. The access request was activated in November 2024, answered on February 17, 2025 and challenged on February 28. Formal investigation began May 5. The revised public report is dated September 4, 2025; Environment says it received the earlier report on July 18 and replied August 28.

The records contain two apparent clerical inconsistencies: November 7 versus November 27 for activation, and request number 25-504 versus 24-504. We use 24-504, which appears in the detailed record and Environment’s response. Neither discrepancy changes the finding; the documents do not resolve them.

## Why these records matter beyond an access dispute

Eagle Gold is about 85 kilometres from Mayo. The mine used a heap-leaching process: cyanide solution passed through piled ore to recover gold.

The [Independent Review Board](https://www.pwc.com/ca/en/car/victoria-gold/assets/victoria-gold-117_020725.pdf) estimated that the failure moved nearly six million cubic metres of ore. Ore and cyanide solution travelled about a kilometre down Dublin Gulch, contaminating local groundwater and Haggart Creek. No one was killed.

The board described several conditions building on one another: ore that drained poorly, water collecting within the pile, problems collecting the cyanide solution and more solution being applied to the ore. It was not a single, simple cause.

Government’s response involved cleanup, regulation and financing. Environment was one part of a wider system that also includes other departments, decision-makers and the Yukon Water Board. The access findings do not make Environment solely responsible for the mine or establish that it hid the cause of the failure.

## The promises and the money need checking too

In April 2026, Yukon [reported progress on 56 review-board recommendations](https://yukon.ca/en/news/yukon-government-provides-update-implementation-independent-review-board-recommendations): 23 implemented, six in progress and 27 expected by spring 2027. Those were the government’s own progress claims, not independently verified results.

In August 2025, Yukon had also [promised work toward more independent mining oversight](https://yukon.ca/en/news/premier-pemberton-announces-early-work-strengthen-independence-yukons-mining-oversight-and-compliance), citing Eagle Gold. The official pages reviewed for this investigation did not establish what became of that specific structural change. The current government can explain what changed in practice and who now makes which decisions.

Then there is the loan. Yukon authorized up to **$220 million** for the receiver. That is a borrowing limit, not a confirmed loss to taxpayers.

The government says its advances have a priority claim over Victoria Gold’s assets and that a sale could allow money to be recovered. The receiver’s May 8, 2026 report said it had not borrowed the full amount. Its August 5 report said a possible Eagle Mine sale to Boroo remained under negotiation, with an exclusive negotiating period extending to October 20.

The sources reviewed do not settle the current amount advanced, interest owed or final recovery. A loan ceiling cannot tell us the final bill.

## The answer should be possible to find

Three questions remain:

- Which disputed records did the requester eventually receive, and what remains withheld?
- How did the watchdog calculate its 14-of-33-pages figure?
- Was the refusal taken to court, and if so, what happened?

For people trying to understand government’s response to a disaster, those are basic questions. The legal reasons for withholding information deserve a fair hearing. So does the public’s right to a clear account of what the access process actually delivered.

An access system should do more than tell a requester they had a good point.
