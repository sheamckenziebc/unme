## Intake and hypothesis

**Lead as received:** The editor supplied a lead based on the Auditor General's 2024 Yukon procurement audit: approximately 47,000 contracts worth more than $1.2 billion; 51% of a representative sample without demonstrated competition or value for money; procurement advice rejected in more than one-third of flagged consultations; contracts signed after work began; and a separate approximately $2-million direct award signed almost a year after work began. The lead also supplied the government's documentation, delivery, fraud and conflict-of-interest response.

**Public-interest reason to investigate:** Procurement determines how public money is spent and whether Yukon and Yukon First Nations businesses receive fair access. The findings remain material because Yukon raised the limits below which contracts may be directly awarded on September 1, 2026.

**Testable hypothesis:** Official records may show that Yukon could not demonstrate market testing in a material share of a representative contract-file sample, that central oversight advice was often rejected without a required rationale or outcome review, and that the government's latest public follow-through remains incomplete or not fully measurable.

**Defeat condition:** The hypothesis would be materially weakened if the audit showed that the 27 files had documented market research, if the sample was not designed to support conclusions about the contract population, or if later authoritative records showed that all recommendations had been independently verified as complete with public outcome metrics.

**Most serious conclusion not currently supported:** The records do not establish corruption, fraud, conflicts of interest, favouritism, a quantified amount of waste, vendor wrongdoing, or that half of the $1.2 billion was misspent or directly awarded.

**Jurisdiction, affected community and responsible decision-makers:** Yukon; residents, taxpayers, businesses and Yukon First Nations; the 16 audited government organizations; deputy heads; and the Department of Highways and Public Works as the central procurement authority. Current policy responsibility rests with the Minister of Highways and Public Works and the department.

## Scope and novelty

**Questions this investigation will answer:**

1. What exactly did the Auditor General test and find?
2. Which conclusions can and cannot be generalized from the representative and targeted samples?
3. What is the strongest documented government response?
4. What did the audit find about direct awards, after-the-fact contracting and rejected central advice?
5. What implementation progress is established by later public records?
6. Why do the September 2026 direct-award threshold increases matter?
7. What immediate, verified steps can readers take?

**Questions outside scope:**

1. Whether any unnamed contract or vendor involved misconduct.
2. Whether an individual public servant acted improperly.
3. Whether a particular contract price was above fair market value.
4. The merits of a contract not examined in the audit.
5. A full outcome evaluation of the Yukon First Nations Procurement Policy.

**Earlier UNME coverage checked:** The investigations collection and public case-file directory were searched September 10, 2026. No previous production investigation covers this audit. The fictional `north-river-contract` demonstration was not treated as evidence.

**Relevant outside reporting checked:** Search results were used for discovery, but the investigation relies on the audit, legislative committee materials and current government records rather than repeating secondary reports.

**What is genuinely new:** The article separates the audit's three denominators, tests the government's documentation defence against the audit methodology, places the 2024 findings beside the September 2026 direct-award increases, and audits the latest public implementation record without claiming that missing public evidence proves inaction.

**Strongest fair account of the criticized party's position:** Yukon says the central problem was missing documentation rather than a defective purchasing process. All reviewed deliverables met the stated needs and were received, spending was below contract ceilings, no fraud or conflicts were found in the reviewed files, and remote or rural markets can limit competition. The government accepted all recommendations, changed guidance and conflict declarations, started data and monitoring work, and says higher direct-award limits reduce administrative cost and improve access for Yukon businesses.

**What the criticized party won, reasonably argued or corrected:** All 53 representative contracts used an acquisition method allowed by the applicable dollar threshold. Every reviewed contract passed the audit's other value-for-money elements. The 27-file finding was triggered by absent market-research evidence, not a finding that prices were inflated or deliverables missing. HPW advice was advisory and deputy heads could lawfully depart from it in the government's best interests. The high-value example came from a risk-selected sample and cannot be generalized.

**What no competent authority found:** No reviewed authority found corruption, fraud, favouritism, bribery, vendor wrongdoing, personal misconduct, a conflict of interest in a sampled file, a quantified loss, or that 51% of the $1.209 billion was misspent.
